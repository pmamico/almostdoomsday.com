Serpukhov-15 · Oko early warning 25.09.1983 · 23:41:00
Briefing · standing file

Your post

Serpukhov-15: a closed military town about a hundred kilometres south of Moscow, and the command centre of Oko, the Soviet satellite early-warning system. It is the night of 25–26 September 1983 and you are the officer of the watch. The floor of consoles below you reports to you; you report upward.

Oko sits in a high orbit and watches the American missile fields against the black of space, looking for the infrared plume of a launch. The system is new. It tells you that something was launched. It does not tell you what it means.

Your orders

Every launch detection goes up the chain, at once. You are not asked whether the detection is correct. You are asked to pass it on, and the assessment is made above you, at the General Staff, in the minutes that follow.

A report from this console launches nothing by itself: corroboration and a political decision stand between it and any retaliation. But the chain begins here, and at these speeds nobody further up has time to go back and check the beginning.

The second opinion

Ground radar is the other half of the system. The satellite sees the plume from orbit; the radar sees the missile itself, once it climbs above the horizon. A satellite reading becomes a track when the radar agrees with it. That agreement — corroboration — is the difference between a reading and a fact.

It cuts both ways. Early in a flight, at long range, the radar would not have seen anything yet even if the launch were real. Silence from the radar is not proof that nothing was fired.

Where things stand

On 1 September, three weeks ago, a Soviet fighter shot down Korean Air Lines flight 007 after it strayed into Soviet airspace. Two hundred and sixty-nine people died. Washington calls it a massacre; Moscow calls it a provocation. Relations are the worst in twenty years.

The leadership in Moscow is working on the assumption that a NATO first strike is a live possibility, and intelligence officers abroad have standing orders to watch for its warning signs. On this console, tonight, an American launch would not read as impossible.

The scores are my assessment of the accounts cited for each incident. I use the five factors in the table, give each a number up to its limit, and add the points together. On every factor, more points mean greater danger. Each event shows the breakdown so you can see how the total was reached.

A score of 70 does not mean a 70% chance of nuclear war. These numbers are a way to compare the incidents on this site, not a calculation of what would have happened. A few points between two cases should not be read as a precise difference in danger.

The same goes for the four bands. They are reading aids, and their boundaries are drawn for readability, not measured: a case at 85 sits in a different band from one at 86, but it is not meaningfully safer.

The weights affect the comparison. Misleading information and wider conflict account for up to 40 points, so a false warning during a crisis can score above a serious weapons accident in peacetime. That reflects the risk of a mistake spreading into a wider conflict. The breakdown helps show why different kinds of incident receive similar totals.

The references support the historical account; the points are my judgement. Some cases have detailed official records, while others rely on recollections made years later. A warning, aircraft put on alert, or use of a nuclear command terminal does not by itself establish that a launch order was close. Where the record allows, each account sets out the decisions and safeguards that still stood before nuclear use.

About the interactive story

Two of the three alerts are reconstructions. The Vandenberg test and the Pacific thermal event are not documented incidents. They are typical duty traffic, written to show what an ordinary night at Serpukhov-15 looked like: the satellite sees something, the ground radar agrees, you pass it on. Only the third alert is a real event.

The third one happened. Just after midnight on 26 September 1983 the Oko system reported an American launch, then four more, at its highest confidence rating and with no radar corroboration. Stanislav Petrov, the officer on duty, reported it as a system malfunction. The cause was sunlight reflecting off high-altitude cloud into the satellite sensors.

The escalation sequence is speculative and marked as such. If you report the attack, the site shows you a counterfactual. A single report from Serpukhov-15 would not have launched anything: corroboration and a political decision stood between that console and any release order, and Soviet doctrine did not launch on satellite data alone. The sequence compresses steps that really existed. It is there to make the stake legible, not to claim that is what would have happened.

Petrov said it himself. He did not think he had saved the world, and he resisted being described that way. What the record shows is narrower and more useful: a warning system that was confidently wrong, and a procedure that expected a person to pass that confidence upward.
